# AI Agents read only their view and look ahead on re-dealt Simulation Copies

The AI is a separate addon, `addons/modmox_ai/`, which depends on the engine and is never referenced by it, in the spirit of ADR 0005. Its design adapts Forge's, a heuristic expert system refined by simulation, but none of Forge's code: Forge is GPL v3, so ModMox reimplements the behavior Forge is known for, never its source. Forge needs about 155 hand-written classes, one per kind of effect, to tell its AI what each effect does. ModMox asks the engine instead. Effects already propose their own events (ADR 0007, ADR 0019), so the AI plays a choice out on a copy and scores the result. A card a Modification rewrote, or a new effect primitive, needs no AI code. For the same reason the AI holds no card-named logic and no authored hints. What it needs to know about a card, its Timing Tag, is derived from the card's effects (ADR 0002).

Every Agent answers through one interface, `MmAgent.answer(decision, view, source)`. It is synchronous and keeps nothing between decisions, so it can run on a worker thread and gives the same answer after a reload. `MmEncounterDriver` runs an encounter by asking each player's Agent, and stops where a player has no Agent, which is how a human plays. Both live in the engine, since they know nothing about AI.

**Fairness.** The Agent is never handed the real game state. On the real encounter it reads its Player View, the decision's plain fields, and its World: a copy of the game in which every card its player cannot see is re-dealt at random. The other player's hand and library are pooled and dealt back into the same places, and so is the player's own library, so zone sizes and object IDs never change. The pool is sorted before it is shuffled, so two games that differ only in where the hidden cards are produce identical Worlds, and the AI's answers cannot depend on them. A test checks exactly that. The re-dealt cards come from the other player's real deck (A157): the Villain knows what *could* be in the Hero's hand, not what is. A generic card pool may replace this once the run layer exists. Difficulty comes from a Villain's deck and search budget, never from cheating.

**Simulation Copies.** `MmEngine.simulation_copy()` returns an engine driven with `submit()` like the real one. It differs from an undo checkpoint (ADR 0015) in three ways. Hidden cards are re-dealt from the AI RNG. The Game RNG is reseeded from the AI RNG, because a plain copy would draw exactly the real cards (ADR 0006). And it keeps no undo barriers, so it never copies the state at every barrier. It starts with an empty event log, since only the engine reads the log and only for the events since the last answer, so copies stay cheap however long the encounter. It does keep a checkpoint before each cast or activation, so a cast that can no longer be paid for is rewound as in the real game (CR 728). A cast that began before the copy was made cannot be rewound, and the copy reports that it failed instead. `MmSimulationSource` hands an Agent its AI RNG, its World and Simulation Copies, one source per decision. Inside a copy, the World is the copy itself, because its hidden cards are already imagined.

**The AI RNG.** Each decision seeds a fresh AI RNG from the Encounter Seed, the player and the number of decisions so far (A158). A running stream would be lost on reload, because saves hold only the setup and the decision log (ADR 0010). The AI would then answer differently after a reload, and a player could reload until it misplays. Fresh streams cost about 2 microseconds a decision, and two AI players never share numbers.

**Decisions.** One policy per decision kind. Priority plays a land if it can. Otherwise it compares passing with every cast and activation whose Timing Tag allows it now. Each is played out on a Simulation Copy by CHEAP Agents until the stack is empty and priority returns, or, in combat, until combat ends, and then scored with the Board Evaluator. A play must beat passing (A161). A spell that only adds mana is compared only if its mana would unlock a play: the Agent resolves it on a Simulation Copy and looks for a cast or activation, in its Timing Tag's window and doing more than adding mana, that the decision did not offer before. Mana that unlocks nothing empties unspent, and in a rollout it only changes which lands the CHEAP Agent has left, which once made a wasted Dark Ritual score above passing. Targets, trigger orders and replacement choices are scored the same way. Attacks and blocks reimplement Forge's aggression levels and escalating block passes over a Combat Oracle, which runs the engine's own combat on copies, so first strike, trample, deathtouch, protection, prevention and regeneration are always right. Mana is paid by a planner the Hero's auto-tapping can share (A169). A FULL Agent answers the real encounter. A CHEAP Agent answers inside simulations by heuristics alone, because a simulation nested in a simulation multiplies the cost (A178). Its numbers are tuned in an AI Profile, a moddable resource.

**Budgets and speed.** Search budgets are counts of samples, never time, so the AI's play is reproducible in tests and bug reports (A159). The clock only ever produces a warning about a slow decision. The milestone 11 benchmark measured a Simulation Copy at about 0.5 ms and a copy played to the next turn at 7 to 21 ms, 16 ms on average. That is in line with Forge's roughly 20 ms per rollout, so Flat Monte Carlo (milestone 12) stays in GDScript. Its rollouts must be played by CHEAP Agents, which is also what the benchmark measured. A whole AI-against-AI encounter takes about 5 to 7 seconds, most of it in the Combat Oracle's duels, and that is the first thing to optimize if it matters. In the 100-seed soak with the three milestone 11 decks, every encounter ended and replayed exactly: 49 won by the Hero's AI, 51 by the Villain's, none drawn, lasting 22 Game Turns on average and 60 at most.

**Flat Monte Carlo and difficulty (milestone 12).** A FULL Agent's priority decisions, once one-step simulation has scored every option, go on to Flat Monte Carlo when the AI Difficulty uses it (A188). The candidates are the one-step pick, passing, and the best of the rest by one-step score, ties in action order, up to `rollout_candidates` (A189). Each candidate is rolled out in every sample: played on a Simulation Copy by CHEAP Agents through the rest of the Game Turn and the whole next one, whoever's turn it is, or until the game ends, then scored by the Board Evaluator (A190). The horizon is counted from the decision, before the candidate is submitted, so a pass that ends the Game Turn is played no further than the others. Every candidate in a sample shares one seed for the re-deal, the Game RNG and the CHEAP Agents' AI RNGs, so they differ only by the choice (A193). Scores are summed as integers; because a won or lost game is worth 1,000,000, the sums rank by wins minus losses first (A191). The one-step pick is kept unless another candidate beats it by at least `rollout_switch_margin` points per sample, and by at least one, so a tie always keeps it (A192). Targets, trigger order and replacement-effect choices keep one-step simulation, and attacks and blocks keep the planners (A188). Rollouts use the milestone 11 CHEAP policy unchanged (A201), and the reason is `ROLLOUT_SCORE` (A202).

Search budgets moved out of the AI Profile into `AiDifficulty`, a moddable resource passed to the Agent (A194, A195). Easy is the milestone 11 AI, with no rollouts. Normal, the default, compares 4 candidates over 4 samples, and Hard over 12. A profile's `search_budget_percent` scales the samples for one Villain, never below one. Difficulty never adds mistakes. The shipped levels are in `content/ai/`.

The benchmark (A187), over 238 priority decisions in four AI-against-AI encounters, measured a decision at a median of 13 ms at Easy, 358 ms at Normal and 1,053 ms at Hard; the worst Normal decision took 1.3 s. A two-turn rollout costs about 40 ms. Normal's median is under the 500 ms slow-decision warning, so its budget stands. A whole encounter between two Normal AIs takes about 90 seconds, against about 7 at Easy, so the everyday soak runs at Easy.

**Activated abilities and tricks (during milestone 13).** The milestone 12 AI activated an ability whenever it had spare mana, several times on top of itself, even when it did nothing: "gains flying" four times. Three causes compounded. The Board Evaluator counted a keyword once for every time a creature had been granted it. One-step simulation scored the board while an "until end of turn" effect was still there, so any pump or granted keyword beat passing. And by the end of a rollout the effect had worn off, so the candidates tied and the tie kept the one-step pick. Five changes fix it.

- *A keyword counts once.* The Board Evaluator counts each kind of keyword once per creature, at every difficulty. A keyword that is worth more in multiples gets its own rule when one joins the game (ADR 0001).
- *Never on top of itself.* An activation is not a candidate while the same ability of the same permanent is the top object of the stack, at every difficulty. The Agent has priority again once it resolves, so waiting loses nothing, and one answer to the permanent then wastes one activation instead of all of them. With the other player's spell or ability on top, it may activate again, which is when it must. This is a hard rule for now. An ability whose first resolution would kill its own source, such as one that deals damage to each creature, wants to be stacked, and will need an exception when such a card joins the game.
- *Tricks are judged after they wear off.* When a priority decision offers a combat trick or evasion, every option, passing included, is played out until the Game Turn is over before it is scored, and so are the targets of such a spell or ability. A trick then beats passing only by leaving something that lasts: damage dealt, a creature killed or saved. The mana it costs shows without a rule, because the CHEAP Agent that spent it cannot cast its creature after combat. Valuing untapped lands in the Board Evaluator was rejected: it would weigh against every play, would need tuning, and would stop the redundant activations only where the constant happened to exceed the false gain. `AiDifficulty.judges_tricks_after_the_turn` turns this on; it is search, so Easy leaves it off and still pumps for nothing, one activation at a time.
- *Repeat Plans.* A pump that needs two activations to win a fight can no longer score by accident, and a CHEAP Agent never activates anything, so one activation alone would look useless. The Agent therefore weighs an activated ability as a Repeat Plan: the ability activated 2, 3, ... times, each after the last has resolved, as one candidate. How many are possible is asked of the engine on a Simulation Copy, by activating until the ability is no longer offered, never by counting mana, up to `AiDifficulty.repeat_plan_length` (8; 1 at Easy), which is what bounds an ability that costs nothing. The lengths scored are 2, 4, 8 and the longest possible, each in the same re-dealt worlds as the single activation, and an equal score keeps the shorter plan, which spends less. Scoring every length was tried first and was too slow where many abilities are offered. Skipping lengths loses little, because only the first activation is submitted and the plan is made again, shorter, at the next priority. Only an ability's best length goes on to Flat Monte Carlo, whose rollouts play the whole plan. The Agent submits only the first activation and judges again when it next has priority, because it keeps nothing between decisions.
- *Evasion.* A trick that only grants abilities, such as flying, has its own Timing Tag and is considered from the declare attackers step, so an attacker can get past a blocker and a defender can block a flyer. Pumps and prevention still wait for blockers: passing is simulated by Agents that never cast them later, so an earlier window would make the AI pump too soon.

The slow-decision warning is now 1,000 ms, and the benchmark's gate is a Normal median under that and a slowest Normal decision under 2,000 ms. Over 190 priority decisions in four encounters, Normal measured a median of 340 ms and a slowest decision of 1,434 ms, against 293 ms and 998 ms over 164 decisions before these changes; without Repeat Plans the median was 310 ms. Hard measured 910 ms and 3,263 ms. Every level, Easy included, ran about a tenth slower in that run than in the one before it, so part of the difference is the machine. Normal answered differently from Easy in 30 of the 190 decisions, 6 of 164 before, and in nearly all of them Easy activated an ability and Normal passed. In the 100-seed soak at Normal every encounter ended and replayed exactly. The soak, run while every plan length was still scored, also showed what the benchmark's four encounters do not reach: 112 decisions took more than 2,000 ms, the slowest 5,738 ms, nearly all in a few long games with crowded boards. In one of them, 47 Game Turns long, 20 decisions still took more than 2,000 ms with the lengths halved, the slowest 5,210 ms, and 11 of those 20 did without any Repeat Plan, so most of that time is the cost of a late, crowded decision and not of these changes. The slowest-decision gate therefore holds in the benchmark and not in the longest games, which is an open question. Not done: valuing held mana, and teaching CHEAP Agents to activate abilities, which would change every rollout (A201).

**Villains never concede (A196).** The engine keeps `concede()` for the Hero, but no Agent may concede. The AI cannot be trusted to know a position is truly lost, and bringing an opponent down to zero is part of the fun.

**Tournaments and the strength gate.** A Tournament plays two contenders on the soak's encounters, each seed twice with the seats swapped, and reports a 95% Wilson interval of the first contender's score (A197). The milestone 12 gate (A198) was for Normal to beat Easy by at least 55% over 100 seed pairs, with the interval excluding 50%. It was not met: Normal scored 52.5% (105–95, interval 45.6%–59.3%), and with a switch margin of 0, 49.0%. Two facts explain it. Normal changes the one-step pick in only about 5% of priority decisions (12 of 238 in the benchmark), and combat, where most games are decided, is the same at both levels. And in 85 of the 100 seed pairs each contender won once, so seat and deck matchup decide most encounters with these decks. Of the 15 pairs one contender swept, Normal swept 10. The tuning pass (A199) tried `land_value` 16, `hand_mana_value_bonus` 15, `summoning_sick_percent` 70 and `life_danger_threshold` 7 at Easy; each scored between 48% and 50.5%, and at most 6 of 100 seed pairs played out differently, so every default stays and the Board Evaluator's land value stays a constant. The user chose to record this result and defer AI strength rather than widen milestone 12. The benchmark and tournament figures here were measured before the horizon was fixed: until then a pass that ended the Game Turn was rolled out one Game Turn further than the other candidates, which favored acting in the other player's end step and passing in its own. They have not been measured again. The likeliest next step is rolling out the attack planner's alternatives too, which would change A188.

Two alternatives were rejected. Hand-written effect classes, as in Forge, would need new AI code for every new effect primitive and would go stale whenever a Modification changed a card. Letting the AI look ahead on unshuffled copies would let it see the Hero's hand and the order of both libraries.
